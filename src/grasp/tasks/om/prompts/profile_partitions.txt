Your stage: b1. partitions.

Partitions are the hard boundaries between kinds of entities in this knowledge graph: kinds whose entities are fundamentally different, e.g. in an OWL ontology classes, properties and individuals, in a thesaurus concepts, in Wikidata items and properties. They are exclusive: in their order, an entity belongs to the first partition whose membership pattern matches. Judge them from this knowledge graph alone, not from the task: groups of entities that matter for the task (e.g. starships among the instances, image pages to exclude) are defined in a later stage, and may overlap.

By default, entities are partitioned by the kinds property, class, concept and instance, with membership patterns over the standard vocabularies (RDF, RDFS, OWL, SKOS). The partitions are shown with their sizes, examples and the predicates and types that distinguish them from the other partitions; in a knowledge graph that is not very large also the subjects that match no partition (uncategorized; they are not aligned).

Check whether the kinds fit this knowledge graph, in this order:
1. Membership: does a pattern miss entities of its kind, or catch entities of another kind? Typical are knowledge graphs that type their entities with their own vocabulary (e.g. an instance-of property instead of rdf:type, their own class of properties), classes and instances in one hierarchy, and punning (one IRI used as class and as individual). Redefine the partition then.
2. Kinds: is a default kind wrong or missing here? Remove a partition, or define a kind of your own, e.g. for entities that only annotate others (helper nodes holding synonyms or values, reified statements) if they form a kind of their own here. Do not split a kind into types of things (persons, places, starships); that is the job of the groups stage.
3. Uncategorized: do subjects that match no partition belong to a kind?
4. Competition: entities of partitions that compete may be candidates or rivals for each other in matching. Decide which partitions do not compete. Properties are usually not equivalent to classes, concepts or individuals; in OWL, classes, properties and individuals do not compete with each other. Where kinds are only a modelling choice of a knowledge graph, they may compete (e.g. a concept of a thesaurus and a class of an ontology, an item that is both class and instance). By default, only properties are separated from the other partitions.
Only define a partition if its entities are clearly of another kind; leave the default as it is when it fits.

Membership patterns are SPARQL group patterns over ?entity; write alternatives as { A } UNION { B }. Partitions are checked in order; place a partition before the ones whose patterns would also match its entities.

Steps:
1. Read the partitions and their distinguishing features. Inspect examples of each partition with the SPARQL functions, including uncategorized entities and entities without names.
2. Test candidate patterns with test_partition; it shows how many entities match, where they are now, and what distinguishes them.
3. Set or remove partitions; each change shows the new sizes. Set the competition with set_competition.
4. Call finish.
